LLM-Based Volunteer Coordination for Crowd Evacuation 
A Comparison with a Game-Theoretic Model 
施品光 
Student ID: 110652012
Advisor: Wong, S. K. 
Abstract 
Volunteer coordination can affect evacuation efficiency when obstacles obstruct access to exits. This study
investigates an approach that uses a large language model (LLM) to support volunteer decisions within a
grid-based crowd evacuation simulator. The approach replaces the game-theoretic volunteer decision
module while retaining the surrounding movement, environment, and simulation framework. Evacuation
context, including nearby pedestrians, visible obstacles, candidate volunteers, distances, and estimated
obstacle impact, is used to inform decisions. Experiments cover four agent populations (60, 90, 120, and
150), with 30 runs per scenario. Performance is evaluated by T90, the number of simulation steps required
for 90% of pedestrians to evacuate. The reported plots show lower T90 values for the LLM-based approach
at all evaluated populations, with a larger difference at higher populations. Natural-language explanations
provide an additional record of the contextual factors associated with volunteer decisions. These findings
indicate potential benefits within the evaluated simulation setting; statistical significance and operational
performance require further evaluation. 
Keywords:   crowd evacuation; volunteer coordination; large language models; cellular automata; game theory. 
1 Introduction 
Crowd evacuation simulation supports the study of pedestrian movement during emergencies such as fires
and earthquakes. Cellular automata (CA) and floor-field methods provide a discrete representation of
pedestrian movement toward exits. When obstacles block evacuation routes, movement alone may not
restore access; pedestrians may also need to volunteer to remove obstructions.
The framework developed by Lin and Wong [1] incorporates obstacle removal and game-theoretic
pedestrian behavior. Building on this setting, the present study examines whether contextual LLM
reasoning can improve volunteer coordination relative to a game-theoretic decision model. The comparison
focuses on the volunteer decision module while retaining the other simulation components.
The contributions are an LLM-based volunteer decision framework, a comparison with the game-theoretic
baseline under a shared simulation design, and an examination of both evacuation performance and natural-
language decision explanations.

2 Background and Baseline Method 
Lin and Wong [1] integrate cellular automata and game theory into an evacuation model that considers
obstacle   removal.   Their   framework   includes   mechanisms   for   pedestrian   conflict   resolution   and
volunteering, together with an anticipation floor field. This study uses the obstacle-removal setting as the
basis for comparing volunteer decision approaches. 
2.1 Game-theoretic volunteer coordination 
In the baseline process, pedestrians observe nearby obstacles and visible agents during a volunteer selection
step. A volunteer decision model estimates each pedestrian’s willingness to help. Potential volunteers are
grouped by the obstacles they can assist, and the nearest candidate is selected for each obstacle. Other
pedestrians continue evacuating. Selected volunteers move toward their assigned obstacles and remove
them, restoring access to affected routes. Between selection steps, current assignments are preserved.

Figure 1 shows the sequence used for the baseline comparison. The distinction between selection steps and
action execution separates the decision to volunteer from the movement and obstacle-removal stages shared
by the two approaches.

3 Proposed LLM Volunteer Coordination 
3.1 Context and decision inputs 
The proposed framework uses evacuation context to inform volunteer decisions. At each selection step,
visible obstacles and nearby pedestrians are identified, and candidate information is prepared for the LLM.
Table 1 summarizes the inputs described in the framework. 
Table 1. Context supplied for LLM-based volunteer decisions. 
Input   Description 
Visible obstacles   Obstacles within the observation range
Nearby pedestrians   Pedestrians around each obstacle
Available volunteers   Candidates available to assist
Distance   Distance between a candidate and an obstacle
Obstacle impact   Estimated influence of removal on evacuation 
The intended coordination process considers candidate proximity and obstacle impact when assigning
volunteers. Its natural-language output records contextual reasons associated with the decision, allowing
volunteer behavior to be examined alongside evacuation performance.

Figure 3 preserves the proposed workflow. At a selection step, the framework checks whether the LLM is

All branches return to volunteer movement and action execution. Assigned volunteers approach and clear

continuous dependence on remote inference; its use should be recorded when evaluating the decision
policy.

4.2 Evaluation measures 
The primary measure is T90, the number of simulation steps required for at least 90% of the initial
population to evacuate. Lower T90 indicates faster evacuation in simulation steps. Relative improvement is
defined as 100 × (T90 baseline   − T90 LLM ) / T90 baseline . This measure does not include remote inference
latency or monetary cost.

6 Discussion 
6.1 Potential benefits 
The evaluated framework suggests that contextual reasoning may support volunteer decisions in evacuation
environments with obstacles. Considering distance, pedestrian density, and estimated obstacle impact gives
the decision policy information about both the feasibility and potential benefit of volunteering. The reported
trend at larger agent populations motivates further study of how these factors influence evacuation
efficiency.
The proposed coordination design aims to consider multiple candidates and obstacles when forming
assignments. However, a lower T90 alone does not demonstrate that global coordination is responsible for
the result. An ablation study comparing local decisions, coordinated assignments, and different context
inputs would help determine which components contribute to performance.
Natural-language explanations offer a readable record for reviewing volunteer choices. They can help
identify unexpected decisions and support analysis of the supplied context. Their usefulness should be
assessed separately from numerical evacuation performance, including whether the explanations are
consistent with the recorded state and final action. 
6.2 Limitations and reproducibility 
Remote LLM calls incur API cost and inference latency. Because T90 is measured in simulation steps,
faster simulated evacuation does not necessarily imply lower wall-clock execution time. The quality of
decisions may also depend on the selected model and prompt. Model identity, prompt content, inference
settings, request limits, and fallback usage should therefore accompany a reproducible evaluation.
The present comparison is limited to a grid-based environment and the evaluated populations. Random
obstacle placement introduces variation, and the available figures do not report per-run distributions,
matched random seeds, or uncertainty estimates. Future experiments should document these conditions,
clarify how obstacle counts are paired or aggregated, and distinguish remote LLM decisions from fallback
decisions. 
7 Conclusion and Future Work 
This study examined LLM-based volunteer coordination within a crowd evacuation simulation and
compared it with a game-theoretic baseline. The reported results show lower T90 values for the LLM-based
approach across the evaluated agent populations, with larger differences at higher populations. Natural-
language explanations additionally support qualitative inspection of volunteer behavior. These observations
motivate further evaluation of contextual decision policies under controlled and reproducible conditions.
Future work should evaluate more complex environments, including dynamic obstacles and changing
evacuation conditions. Comparisons among different LLMs and locally deployed models could examine
inference cost and response time. Statistical analysis of repeated runs, ablation experiments, and validation
using human behavioral or real-world evacuation data would help assess robustness and practical relevance.
